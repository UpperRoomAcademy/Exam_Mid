The Secret Light of the Deep Sea

The deep ocean is one of Earth's most mysterious places. Sunlight cannot reach below about 650 feet, so most of the deep ocean is completely dark. Yet many creatures that live there produce their own light — a process called bioluminescence (by-oh-loo-mih-NES-ents).

Bioluminescence happens through a chemical reaction inside an animal's body. Special proteins called luciferins mix with oxygen to create a soft glow. The light is usually blue or green because those colors travel farthest through seawater.

Deep-sea animals use bioluminescence in several clever ways. The anglerfish, for example, dangles a glowing lure above its head to attract prey in the pitch-black water. Firefly squid flash bright patterns to confuse predators. Some species of jellyfish emit a steady blue glow that may help them communicate with each other.

Scientists have discovered that over 75% of deep-sea creatures are capable of producing their own light. Despite this, humans have explored less than 20% of the ocean floor. Researchers believe that many bioluminescent species are still waiting to be discovered. Each deep-sea expedition brings new surprises, reminding us that our own planet still holds extraordinary secrets.
